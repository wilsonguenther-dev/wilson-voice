# Yap Changelog

## v0.8.0

- **Yap is a paid app now, and the trial is a real one.** Every install gets 14 days of the whole app, no card and no account. A $29 one-time license (a $19 founding price runs against the same purchase) turns it on for good, on up to three of your Macs.
- **When the trial ends, only new dictation stops.** Your history, your search, your exports, your settings, your dictionary and your snippets keep working forever. The words you already spoke are yours — Yap does not hold them hostage to sell you a license, and pressing the hotkey out of habit gets a sentence explaining that, not a dead key.
- **Your license is checked on your Mac, not on a server.** Activation verifies an Ed25519 signature against a key compiled into the app. There is no account, no login, no phone-home on the dictation path, and no license check that can fail because you are on a plane. The single network call Yap's licensing makes is a background refresh of the refund list, and every way it can fail — offline, DNS, a bad response — leaves your license exactly as it was.
- **A refunded license stops working; nobody else's does.** Refunds and chargebacks revoke one specific license, never a batch, and a revoked license that is contested and won comes straight back.
- **You can always see where you stand, and Yap only says it once.** A small chip in the header carries your trial or your license at all times, and it is the only place the countdown lives — there is exactly ONE message about the trial running out, three days before it does, and it never comes back. No daily countdown, no launch nag, no interstitial.
- **Settings → License.** One screen with the state of this Mac, a Buy button that opens Stripe's own checkout in your browser, and a box to paste the key you already own. Bad keys get a sentence that tells you what to do next instead of the word "invalid", and "Remove from this Mac" moves a seat to another machine.
- **The terms, the privacy policy and the price now say the same thing the app does.** The Terms of Use describe what $29 actually buys — personal use, three Macs, every future update, no renewal — alongside the fourteen-day trial, a no-questions fourteen-day refund, and a plain paragraph about recording other people, which is your call and your responsibility because Yap does not announce itself to anyone in the room. The Apache-2.0 carve-out now names the **shipped release** rather than a version number the working tree still claimed — the v0.7.0 release (tag `v0.7.0`, commit `8c8740f`) and everything before it stay Apache-2.0 for everyone who received them, and every commit after it, licensing and payment code included, is proprietary. The in-repo version moved to **0.8.0** so a clone can no longer read as "this IS 0.7.0, so the Apache file covers it", and `NOTICE` and the README carry the same cut-off so the Apache file is never encountered alone. The privacy policy now covers the purchase too: Stripe handles the payment, we keep a one-way hash of your email and the license's own metadata, and the licensing path's single network call downloads a public refund list that carries no key, no email and no query. It also stops claiming Yap never keeps audio, because YV52 does: a take that fails or is rejected keeps its recording in a local recovery folder for up to 7 days so you can retry it, Discard deletes it on the spot, and the policy now says exactly that in all three places it used to say the opposite.
- **The site has a price on it.** A pricing section states $29 once, yours forever, three Macs, refundable for fourteen days, with the founding $19 code described honestly — a real 500-person cap and no countdown timer, because there is no countdown. The "free and open source" line is gone from the page it outlived.
- **The end of the trial is an offer, not a wall.** Pressing your hotkey after day 14 brings up a warm prompt with Yappy on it: buy, or paste a key. Dismiss it and the app behind it still works — your notes and history stay yours forever, and the prompt says so.
- **Auto-update is live.** The updater keypair now has a retrievable password backing it, so this release is the first one signed and verifiable end to end — installed Macs on v0.7.0 and later can update in place instead of a manual DMG reinstall.
- **Yappy keeps talking for the whole take again.** Live commentary had gone quiet partway through longer dictations; it now escalates and reacts for the full duration, not just the opening beat.
- **Lighter when you're not dictating.** The ASR model now loads lazily — Yap idles with nothing armed and the first dictation of a session spins the engine up — and an energy pass parked idle pill animations, audited polling for busy timers, and unloads the polish sidecar when it isn't in use.

## v0.7.0

- **Never lose a dictation.** Every take is written to a crash-safe capture journal before it is transcribed, so a dictation now survives the app dying mid-take and is offered back to you on the next launch. One database transaction per dictation means a saved transcript can no longer be reported as a failed take, or land twice.
- **Rejections are recoverable, not deleted.** When the hallucination gate or a mid-take mic drop kills a transcript, the audio is kept and the take stays retryable instead of vanishing. The gate itself is now windowed and length-aware, so it stops eating long, legitimate dictations while still catching the classic Whisper filler loops.
- **The two crashes people actually hit are gone.** Cmd-Q now releases the ASR Metal device before exit instead of tearing it out from under the GPU, and quitting mid-transcription drains the in-flight engine lease first. Both used to be reproducible crashes.
- **Yappy talks through the whole take again.** Live commentary keeps reacting for the full duration of a dictation — escalating props and tone-aware lines as you talk — rather than going quiet after the opening beat.
- **Drag the pill where you want it.** Grab the pill and drag it to a screen edge to dock it, Wispr-style, by direct manipulation instead of hunting through settings.
- **An honest paste receipt.** Yap only tells you the text was pasted after it observes a real read receipt from the target app. If the paste did not land, you are told, and the transcript is still in your history.
- **Lighter on memory and disk.** Bounded audio buffers, a startup and daily garbage collection pass over stale artifacts, and memory telemetry in Diagnostics — Yap no longer grows without limit across a long day of use.
- **Clear History actually destroys the words.** Clearing now securely deletes the rows, rebuilds the search index, and vacuums the database, so cleared transcripts are not recoverable from the file afterwards. And **Export all** streams every transcript you have ever dictated to a JSONL file with a visible row count.
- **Onboarding stops dead-spinning.** Every terminal outcome of a take — success, rejection, error — now reports back, so first-run setup can never hang waiting for an event that never comes. Model setup downloads quietly behind a slim ribbon.
- **Local crash telemetry.** Panics and native macOS crash reports are captured, sanitized of your dictated words, and surfaced in Diagnostics. Nothing is ever uploaded — Yap still makes no outbound connections.

- YV57: List detection that survives real ASR output (rules R7–R11 of `docs/research/wispr-formatting-deep-dive.md`). The v1 detector fired on NONE of Wispr's own documented list examples, for two compounding reasons the deep-dive measured: the cue table held no numeric words (`one`, `two`, `1.`), and cues were only matched at the START of a punctuation-split clause — so an unpunctuated ASR transcript, which is the normal case, is one clause and bailed at `clauses.len() < 2` before it looked at anything. `detect_and_format_list` now scans the TOKEN stream (R8) over a cue table that covers cardinals `one`…`ten`, ordinals `first`…`tenth`, dictated digit markers `1.`…`10.` (the trailing mark is required — a bare "2" is a quantity far more often than a cue) and `number one`…`number ten` (R7), and takes its safety from a monotone sequence rather than from punctuation: a chain must anchor at position 1 and then resolve each next position in turn, so "I want one coffee and a bagel" stays prose because no later "two" exists, and `next`/`finally`/`lastly` may only EXTEND a chain that already resolved 1 and 2, never start one. A two-item chain additionally needs ≥ 2 tokens of content per item, which is what keeps "one coffee and two bagels" out. A cue that opens no item is a quantity rather than an enumerator, so a chain any of whose items begins with a word that cannot start one (`of`, `and`, `that`, `which`, … — articles deliberately excluded, since "first, the report goes out" is a real item) is thrown out too: "one of the things I noticed was that two of the servers were down" satisfies the monotone rule by accident and stays prose. - YV92: Two silent corruptions in the same twelve lines of resampling code, both harmless for a five-second dictation and both fatal for a three-hour lecture. **The first is aliasing.** Capture runs at the device's rate — 48 kHz on a MacBook mic — and everything downstream runs at 16 kHz, and the decimation between them was pure linear interpolation with no lowpass in front of it. Linear interpolation's kernel is a triangle, i.e. a sinc-squared frequency response: it attenuates 10 kHz by about 1.3 dB and 16 kHz by about 3.3 dB before folding the entire 8–24 kHz band down into 0–8 kHz. A close-mic dictation clip has almost nothing up there, which is why nobody ever saw it; a room with HVAC, a fan and a keyboard in it has a great deal, and the folded noise lands directly in the band the recognizer reads — where, per the plan's own finding, the numbers come back bad and the model gets the blame. The fix is an 8th-order Butterworth lowpass at 7.2 kHz ahead of the interpolator, and the order is the part worth arguing about: the plan offered "at absolute minimum a 2nd-order Butterworth", but a single biquad at that corner gives only ~6.7 dB at 10 kHz against an acceptance bar of 20 dB, so it is four cascaded sections instead of one — **28.46 dB measured at 10 kHz**, 46.8 dB at 12 kHz, 65+ dB from 14 kHz up, and 0.000 dB across 100 Hz–3.4 kHz, i.e. the voice is untouched. The negative control is the part that makes it a test rather than a claim: a 10 kHz tone decimated 48→16 kHz by the OLD path reappears as a 6 kHz ghost at full amplitude (0.5000), and by the new one at 0.0188 — **28.5 dB of alias removed** — and on the eval corpus's real 48 kHz speech with broadband noise above 8 kHz, the energy each decimator lets into the speech band differs by **27.8 dB**. Now: a pure state machine (`input_format.rs`, the same shape as `sysaudio::restore_plan` and testable with no audio hardware at all) turns an observation into the exact action the capture path takes — close the current spill segment, write a `device_change` marker carrying the mach host time and the output-sample index where the segment boundary falls, reopen with a ratio built from the NEW nominal rate. A ratio never survives a format change, asserted three steps deep through the real AirPods sequence (48k → 24k → 16k HFP → 48k) and again in the DSP itself, where 0.5 s of input at the new 24 kHz rate must produce ~8000 output samples and not the ~4000 a stale ratio would. Promptness comes from `AudioObjectAddPropertyListener` on the default-input selection and on the live device's stream format and nominal sample rate — the callback records the selector in an atomic and returns, allocating nothing and locking nothing, exactly like an audio callback — and correctness comes from the state machine on the worker thread, so the interesting half needs no microphone to test. A burst of selectors from one renegotiation collapses to one segment, and a zero rate (CoreAudio for "I don't know yet", which is what a Bluetooth link reports mid-negotiation) is ignored rather than installed as a garbage ratio. The device cache is now keyed on name AND format, the take survives the swap (the DSP, its buffers, its AGC statistics and its crash journal are carried onto the reopened stream; if the reopen fails the audio is still kept and Disarm still hands it back), and `LiveStream::has_failed` is polled on a 60-second watchdog instead of only at Arm — the plan's fix (d) — with the listener making a real change arrive on the next tick rather than a minute later. - YV97: Meeting summaries, and two corrections that decide whether the summary is worth reading. **The first is where extraction happens.** The plan ran map-reduce the ordinary way: chunk the transcript, merge the chunks' free text log₂(n) times, then ask the merged prose for the action items. By the time that question is asked the evidence is gone — the exact commitments, the wording, and any way to say which part of the meeting a claim came from — and "preserve every quoted commitment" is an instruction repeated to a 1.5B model at every merge level with nothing enforcing it. So the stages are inverted: actions, decisions and open questions are extracted in MAP, once per chunk, where the segment ids are local and enumerable, and REDUCE only merges the narrative. The action list is merged by dedup, never re-generated, so a merge cannot reword a commitment, drop one, or invent one. **The second is that a grammar constrains shape, not truth.** A JSON schema will happily produce `"segment": "seg_0093"` for a segment that was never in the chunk the model was reading — a plausible id is exactly what a hallucination looks like. So the GBNF is generated per chunk with the evidence field as a literal enum over the ids actually present in that chunk, and the parser drops any citation outside that set anyway, because a grammar this build failed to apply must not silently become an unconstrained decode. - YV101: Yap has been shipping a binary that cannot launch on macOS 12 or 13, and this fixes it. - YV103: The fix the meeting Notetaker's device handling needed *before* it ships, not after somebody loses a lecture to it. When Yap starts capturing the audio of a call, it builds a private aggregate device around whatever your Mac is currently playing sound through. Put AirPods in, wake a Studio Display, plug in headphones — that device moves, and the capture has to be rebuilt around the new one. The obvious way to do that is a documented infinite-loop bug, and it is not hypothetical: Hyprnote, the closest open-source equivalent to this feature, shipped exactly it and had to fix it (fastrepl/hyprnote PR #1654), because creating and destroying an aggregate device makes macOS emit its own device-change notifications — so the listener answers its own rebuild with another rebuild, forever, and no audio is ever captured. Yap's device handling is now a guarded state machine instead of a callback. **It ignores its own noise:** while Yap is creating or tearing down its own capture device, every device notification is discarded, and a rebuild asked for while one is already running is refused rather than tearing the same device down twice. **It waits for the dust to settle:** one AirPods connect is not one notification, it is a burst of four or five across half a second, so the burst is coalesced into exactly one rebuild rather than six overlapping ones. The window opens at the first event of a burst and is never pushed back by later ones, so a device that keeps chattering still gets its rebuild instead of being put off forever, and a pair of AirPods that connects and drops inside the same window is correctly no rebuild at all. **And it is one state machine, not two.** The mid-recording AirPods handling shipped in v0.7.0's dictation path and this one are the same problem seen from two sides, so this is the same object extended — same decisions, same debounce, same guard, one threshold declared in one place — rather than a second listener with its own copy of the invariant that would drift from the first. All of it is proved without any audio hardware, including a 100-round soak that interleaves rebuilds with the notification storm each rebuild sets off; the real CoreAudio listeners are verified installing and removing cleanly on a Mac.
- YV104: There is a documented CoreAudio bug where the system-audio tap keeps running perfectly and delivers nothing but silence — every callback fires on time, every timestamp looks right, and every single sample is a zero, while the call you are recording is plainly still making noise. Apple's own forum thread has a fifty-one minute session on a fanless MacBook Air that lost sixty seconds, then fifty-three, then a hundred and forty-one, and then **sixteen minutes in one stretch**, with nothing anywhere reporting a problem. The plan for the meeting Notetaker was going to read that exact symptom — long silence while something is obviously playing — as "your system-audio permission looks denied", badge the meeting, and give up: a healthy recording marked broken, and a sixteen-minute hole in the middle of a lecture that no badge was ever going to bring back. Yap now does the opposite thing first. Sustained silence on the system-audio track gets a full rebuild — the tap and the private capture device both destroyed and recreated, which is what Apple's thread says is the only thing that actually recovers — and it never, under any circumstances, gets a permission verdict on the first try, however long the silence has run and however incriminating it looks. Those rebuilds are budgeted at three per meeting so a genuinely broken tap cannot spend an hour tearing devices down and rebuilding them, and every single one is written into the meeting itself: when it fired, how long the track had been quiet, why, what else it could plausibly have been, and whether the rebuild worked. A meeting that needed three rebuilds is now something you can look at afterwards rather than something that was quietly worse than it looked. And when the rebuilds are spent, the meeting still tells you the truth rather than a guess. There turns out to be exactly one honest way to tell a real permission denial from this bug, and it is a single bit: **did this track ever deliver one non-silent sample?** A denial is silent from the very first sample and stays that way; the bug only starts after minutes of real audio. So a track that was never once alive says permission looks off, a track that worked and then died says the system audio stopped and could not be restarted, and neither one ever pretends to be the other. Silence has four other innocent explanations — your Mac's output moved to headphones the capture is not pointed at, everyone on the call is muted, and two ways the capture can be built wrong — and all four are recorded alongside the rebuild instead of being quietly ruled out. Whatever happens to the system-audio track, **the meeting never stops and your microphone keeps recording**: losing the room's audio is a banner and a mark in the recording, never the end of the session you are in the middle of.
